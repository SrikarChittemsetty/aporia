The sage, therefore, regulates his assent by the degrees of assurance which experience affords him. In some cases the concurrence of past observation has been entire and uncontradicted: he expects the event with the last confidence, and calls it certain. In others he has found a contrariety in the reports of nature, as in the weather of a season or the recovery of the sick; here he must weigh the instances on either hand, incline to the more numerous, and abate just so much of his persuasion as the contrary trials require him to surrender. Such conclusions never rise above probability, and it is folly to hold a conclusion faster than the trials will bear.
